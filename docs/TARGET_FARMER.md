# The target farmer: who DamDays is for

**Why this page.** On Sat 3 Oct 2026 a mentor advised us to describe the farmer DamDays suits best (land area, stock numbers, yearly turnover, remoteness, number of workers) and to leave the corporate agribusiness case aside for now. This page builds that profile from public farm surveys and checks it against our own data.

**How to read it**

- **Ranges** are averages per farm from public surveys, mostly ABARES's farm survey of broadacre farms (AAGIS), for the three latest years, 2022-23 to 2024-25. Dollars are per farm per year, as ABARES publishes them (adjusted for inflation to recent dollars). ABARES samples about 20 to 100 farms per group and year, so treat any single year as rough; the range across years is the better guide.
- **Derived** marks our own arithmetic on published numbers. **Our data** marks numbers from DamDays's own files, with the file named. **Unverified** marks things we could not confirm from a live source; they are listed at the end and not used in the profile.
- As everywhere in DamDays, "%" means only how full a dam is. Shares are written "N in 10" or "x of y".
- Every source was read on Sat 3 Oct 2026. Links are at the end.

## Meet Kath and Graeme

> **Illustrative.** A made-up family, not real people. Each number is a typical value from the tables below, not a measurement of one farm.

Meet Kath and Graeme. They run sheep and cattle on about 1,500 hectares in NSW Central West, in what the ABS calls Outer Regional Australia: about 2,000 sheep and 250 to 300 cattle, and $500,000 to $700,000 a year through the books. It is a family business. The two of them do most of the work, about 80 to 100 weeks of labour a year counting a little paid help, and Graeme is in his sixties. All their stock water comes from dams: about a dozen, mostly small paddock dams, plus one or two big ones of more than half a hectare of water that carry the stock through summer. There is no bore and no pipeline. In a dry summer one of them checks the water every day, and working out how long each dam will last means measuring it and doing the sums by hand. When a dam fails, every choice costs money: cart water (a daily job), buy feed, agist or sell. In the 2019-20 drought, mixed sheep-and-cattle farms in NSW spent about $114,000 on fodder on average, against $10,000 to $20,000 a year in the last three years. They read a text; they rarely open an app or an email. DamDays sends them one text a week: how many days of water each of their big dams has left.

## The evidence, variable by variable

### Enterprise and where

- ABARES groups livestock farms into **beef specialists**, **sheep specialists** and **sheep-beef** farms (a mix where neither dominates). Nationally there are about 22,100 beef specialists, 8,900 sheep specialists and around 3,000 sheep-beef farms, out of 34,000 broadacre livestock farms (ABARES, *Financial performance of livestock farms 2021-22 to 2023-24*). The survey covers farms with an estimated value of agricultural operations above $40,000.
- In the three states DamDays has been tested in, ABARES counts, for 2024-25: NSW 6,068 beef, 2,985 sheep and 2,493 sheep-beef farms; Victoria 4,100, 3,707 and 651; South Australia 489, 1,282 and 525. That is **about 22,300 farms** in all (derived: our sum; it includes big western properties that are not the target).
- Scale: 53,964 businesses are involved in the cattle industry and 22,474 in the sheep industry (MLA Fast Facts 2025). NSW holds 38 in 100 of Australia's sheep, Victoria 24 and South Australia 15 (MLA). At 30 June 2025 NSW had 5,528,200 beef cattle, Victoria 2,609,700 and South Australia 1,082,600 (ABS, *Australian Agriculture: Livestock 2024-25*, experimental estimates).
- Stock water is surface water. Agriculture Victoria estimates that about 98 in 100 of Victoria's meat and wool production uses unregulated water, and notes that many producers rely on small paddock dams for stock and domestic water (*How much water does my farm need?* and *Managing farm water supplies*, both updated 22 Jul 2026).

- ABARES's turnover groups: small (under $250,000), medium ($250,000 to $750,000), large ($750,000 to $2 million), very large (over $2 million). The target farmer is mostly **medium**, sometimes large.
- Where that sits: ranked by receipts, the middle southern beef farms (the 5th and 6th tenths) average $155,287 and $190,340; the 8th tenth $313,660, the 9th $486,392 and the top tenth $1,502,381 (ABARES, *Disaggregating farm performance statistics by size*, three-year average to 2023-24, 2022-23 dollars). So the target is roughly the **top third** of southern beef farms. The top tenth alone produces 47 in 100 parts of southern beef output.
- Why not smaller farms: from 2020-21 to 2022-23, small livestock farms got most of their household income from outside the farm (ABARES, livestock farms report). For them, the farm's water is a smaller part of the family's money.

### Family operation and workers

- More than 95 in 100 broadacre and dairy farms are family owned and operated (ABARES, farm debt report).
- **Total labour** (all work on the farm by the owner-manager, partners, family, hired permanent and casual workers and sharefarmers; contractors excluded, ABARES definitions): 63 to 107 work weeks a year across the six groups above, 2022-23 to 2024-25. That is about 1.2 to 2 people working full time all year (derived: weeks divided by 52).
- **Hired wages** are small: $6,690 to $7,130 a year on NSW beef farms, $12,410 to $25,580 on NSW sheep-beef farms, $2,170 to $4,530 on Victorian beef farms, $16,610 to $37,350 on Victorian sheep-beef farms. Most of the work is the family's.
- **Owner-manager age:** 57 to 69 across the six groups (2022-23 to 2024-25). The owner-manager worked 36 to 54 hours a week on the farm and the spouse 7 to 18 (2019-20 to 2021-22, the latest years ABARES publishes for hours).
- A real example in our Victorian test region: a 1,000 ha sheep and cattle farm at Branxholme, run by a couple with one full-time employee (Agriculture Victoria case study, *Smart remote monitoring*).

### Remoteness

- **Our data:** of the 1,683 dam-sized waterbodies (mostly farm dams) DamDays forecasts in its two test regions, 1,101 lie in **Outer Regional** Australia, 397 in **Inner Regional** and 185 in **Remote** Australia (about 2 in 3, 1 in 4 and 1 in 9). None are in a Major City. In NSW Central West alone: 474 of 894 Outer Regional, 243 Inner Regional, 177 Remote; in western Victoria / SE South Australia: 627 of 789 Outer Regional, 154 Inner Regional, 8 Remote. (Method: each dam's centre, from `data_cache/attributes.csv`, placed in the ABS Remoteness Areas of the ASGS Edition 3, 2021, using the ABS's generalised boundaries; three points were checked against the full boundaries and agreed.)
- The demo farm points in the app are 2.1 to 20.4 km from the nearest town (`app/data/real/farms.json`). When there were 10 of them, 5 were Inner Regional and 5 Outer Regional; one of those 10, Farm D near Dubbo, was set aside on Sat 3 Oct (its waterbodies are not farm dams), so 9 remain. They are not real homesteads, so this is only a rough guide to distance.
- Our reading: for checking water, the distance that matters is the one **around the farm**, not to town. Water points are spread over hundreds of hectares, and some families run blocks far apart (one Victorian case study: a 485 ha out-block 30 km from the home farm).

### Farm dams: how many, and how many DamDays can see

- **All farm dams.** Australia has 1.765 million farm dams covering 4,678 km² of water; NSW has 654,983 of them and Victoria the highest density, 1.73 dams per km² (Malerba, Wright and Macreadie 2021, *Remote Sensing* 13(2):319).
  - Derived: NSW averages 0.82 dams per km² (654,983 dams over 801,137 km² of NSW, Geoscience Australia). So an average 1,000 ha holds about **8 dams in NSW and 17 in Victoria**. These are averages over all land, farmed or not; there is no census of dams per farm.
  - Derived: the average farm dam covers about **0.27 ha** (4,678 km² over 1.765 million dams), half the smallest dam DamDays can see. A few big dams pull that average up, so most farm dams are smaller still: **most farm dams are too small for DamDays.** The volume table for small rectangular dams on Agriculture Victoria's *Managing farm water supplies* page stops at 20 m x 40 m of water (0.08 ha).
- **Dams DamDays can see.** DamDays forecasts waterbodies that look and behave like farm dams: **6 to 55 Landsat pixels, which is 0.54 to 4.95 ha of water**, wet in at least half of the satellite looks before 2016, and compact in shape ([docs/DATA.md](DATA.md), "dam-like"). Smaller dams are too small to see reliably; bigger waterbodies are usually not farm dams.
  - NSW Central West: 894 such dam-sized waterbodies, mostly farm dams; median 0.81 ha, and 9 in 10 are 2.07 ha or smaller (`app/data/real/forecasts.json`).
  - Western Victoria / SE South Australia: 789 such dam-sized waterbodies, mostly farm dams; median 1.26 ha (`data_cache/attributes.csv`).
  - The filter looks at size, shape and water history, not land use, so it also lets through some town and industrial ponds and short stretches of river (see the next bullets and the [README's limits](../README.md#limits)).
- **How many farms have one (our data, the hard filter).** We have no property boundaries, so we counted around evenly spaced points (one every 0.01 degrees, about 1 km) across each test region's whole box, farmland and not:

| test region | points | with a DamDays dam within 2 km (1,257 ha circle) | within 3 km (2,827 ha circle) | dams near a point that has any |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NSW Central West | 90,000 | 7,897 (about 1 in 11) | 15,461 (about 1 in 6) | usually 1; 9 in 10 have at most 2 (2 km) or 3 (3 km) |
| western Victoria / SE South Australia | 75,000 | 7,102 (about 1 in 11) | 13,175 (about 1 in 6) | the same |

  So the target farmer is the one who **has at least one big dam**, and typically sees 1 or 2 of their dams in the text. Many farms in these regions have none yet. (The share of *grazing farms* with one is probably higher than these whole-box shares, which include forest, crops and towns, but we have not measured it: unverified.)
- **The demo farms are the best case.** The app's demo farms were placed in the middle of the densest clusters of dam-sized waterbodies, at least 25 km apart. The 9 in the app have 5 to 30 such dams within 3 km (median 8; Farm E near Mudgee, the one in the weekly text and the video, has 5). That shows the text well, but a typical target farm sees fewer.
- **Dense clusters also attract waterbodies that are not farm dams.** On Sat 3 Oct we checked every demo farm's 3 km circle against aerial photos. Farm E's 5 dams are all farm dams (a ring tank in an irrigated paddock, a vineyard dam, two paddock dams and a dam on a creek). The Dubbo demo farm (Farm D) was set aside: none of its 7 waterbodies was clearly a farm dam, and they included three cells of a treatment-pond complex, a pond at a racecourse, a pond at the town edge and a stretch of the Macquarie River. Several other demo circles (Farms C, G, H, I and J) also take in mine, wetland, town or treatment-works waterbodies, from whole-circle looks.

### Stock water source

- Victoria: unregulated water is used by about 98 in 100 of meat and wool production. Agriculture Victoria describes the paddock catchment dam as the traditional stock water supply, and notes that many producers rely on small paddock dams (*How much water does my farm need?*; *Managing farm water supplies*).
- The weakness: in recent droughts most farm dams went dry, and a small dam 3 m deep can lose up to three-fifths of its water to evaporation in 12 months (Agriculture Victoria). NSW DPI's rule of thumb: a dam 3 m deep and full at the start of summer may lose about 1 m of it by the end of summer (NSW DPI, *Water for livestock* webinar).
- Agriculture Victoria recommends a large, deep, fenced dam piped to troughs, because a large deep dam can hold 2 to 3 years of good water with little or no refill. Those large dams are the ones DamDays can see.

### How they check dams today

- **By driving.** A family near Cavendish in western Victoria (about 700 cattle and 12,500 sheep on 2,700 ha) checked troughs twice a week outside summer and **every day in summer**, and tanks once a week (Agriculture Victoria, *AgTech provides on-farm labour savings*). The Branxholme farm spent about 3 hours a week from December to March checking tank levels before fitting sensors (*Smart remote monitoring*). Water quality should be checked by eye weekly, and daily in hot weather (Agriculture Victoria, *Drought Feeding and Management of Beef Cattle*, January 2025); NSW DPI says to test water weekly in a drought.
- **By hand calculation.** The official method is to measure each dam's length, width and depth at the water line, look up its volume in a table, allow for evaporation and divide by the stock's daily use. Agriculture Victoria's own example: 420,000 litres divided by 8,000 litres a day is **52 days of water** (*Drought Feeding and Management of Beef Cattle*). NSW DPI teaches the same sum (*Water for livestock*). That number, days of water left in each dam, is what DamDays works out from the satellite record and sends every week.
- **By sensors, where they have them.** Trough and tank sensors cost about $545 per device plus $8 to $10 a month (*Smart remote monitoring* case study); the Cavendish family's four sensors cost $1,972 for a year, including a $50 subscription per device and 10 cents per SMS alert. They measure troughs and tanks, one device per water point, not how long a dam will last. The *Smart remote monitoring* case study reports about half the time saved on checking fences, tanks and troughs.

### What a dry dam costs

- **Carting water.** Agriculture Victoria: carted water is usually needed every day, so carting takes a lot of labour, and pouring it into an earthen dam is generally not feasible because of seepage and evaporation; it should go into a tank and troughs (*Drought Feeding and Management of Beef Cattle*). A beef producer near Coleraine, on buying a truck and tanker to cart water: "It'd be a full-time job, you'd get nothing else done" (ABC News, 16 Feb 2026). A grazier near Griffith with 2,500 Merino ewes had been carting water for two years, since his dams stopped filling (ABC News, 19 Feb 2026).
- **What governments have paid towards it.** NSW's 2018 Drought Transport Subsidy covered up to half the cost of transporting fodder, water for stock and livestock, up to $20,000 per farm business (RDA Central West, 29 Aug 2018). Victoria's Farm Drought Support Grants (guidelines revised July 2025) list water carting for livestock as eligible: up to $5,000 per business statewide and up to $10,000 in south-west Victorian council areas including Southern Grampians, Glenelg, Moyne, Ararat and Pyrenees, matched dollar for dollar. A farmer carting with their own truck is paid $1.50 per km (vehicle under 2.5 t) up to $7.50 per km (the heaviest).
- **One farm's carting bill:** carting can cost about $11,000 a year, for a farmer at Cleve on South Australia's Eyre Peninsula (ABC Rural, 20 Jun 2020). (Outside our test regions; one example, not an average.)
- **Fodder, the bigger bill.** In the 2019-20 drought year, ABARES's average fodder spending per farm was $113,970 on NSW sheep-beef farms (against $10,160 to $19,690 in 2022-23 to 2024-25), $97,900 on NSW beef farms (against $9,640 to $20,080) and $77,550 across all broadacre farms in the NSW Central West region (against $4,650 to $12,560). That is 5 to 17 times a recent year (derived). In the 2024-25 southern drought, fodder spending rose to $43,740 per farm in Southern and Eastern Victoria (from $13,550 in 2022-23) and $48,030 in South Australia's South East (from $19,940).
- **The 2024-26 southern drought, in our test regions:** near Cavendish, many dams went dry on one property, and the family were spending $7,000 to $10,000 or more a week on feed (ABC Rural, 10 May 2025). Near Coleraine, a beef producer had cut his herd to 230 Hereford cows as the river and dams dropped (ABC News, 16 Feb 2026).

### How to reach them

- A mentor told us that farmers rarely open emails or apps, and that a text once a week suits them (mentor feedback, Fri 2 Oct 2026; not yet tried with farmers). With owner-managers aged 57 to 69 and one or two people doing the work, the weekly text is the product; the app is for set-up and a closer look.

## Why DamDays fits this farmer

1. **It watches the water they depend on.** Their stock drink from dams, and the dams that carry them through summer are the big ones (0.54 to 4.95 ha) that DamDays sees. It needs no sensor on the dam and no tape measure. (It does not replace looking at the stock and the water itself: see the [README's limits](../README.md#limits).)
2. **It answers their actual question, every week.** Agriculture Victoria tells farmers to work out each dam's days of water by measuring and dividing. DamDays sends that number, cautious by design: on the ten test years (July 2016 to June 2026) the "at least N days" number held 9 times in 10, across 729,749 forecasts ([artifacts/test_results.md](../artifacts/test_results.md)), and each dam's card shows how often it held on that dam. (The satellite sees how much of a dam is wet, not how deep it is: "a third" means a third of the dam's usual full wet area.)
3. **It saves the scarcest thing on a family farm: time.** One or two people (63 to 107 work weeks a year) cover hundreds of hectares; in a dry summer someone checks water daily. A weekly text tells them which dam to worry about first.
4. **It gives them time to choose.** Carting is a daily, labour-heavy job; fodder bills in a drought year run to tens of thousands of dollars. Knowing weeks ahead which dam will drop below a third leaves time to move stock to the dams that will last, book a water cart or agistment, or sell while there is still a choice.
5. **It reaches them the way they read.** A plain SMS that fits one message, not an app (mentor feedback). No new hardware and nothing to install.
6. **It has been tested where they farm.** NSW Central West, and western Victoria / SE South Australia, mostly Outer Regional country. On the latest satellite looks (to 14 Sep 2026), 272 of the 894 dam-sized waterbodies DamDays tracks in NSW Central West (mostly farm dams) were already below a third full (`app/data/real/meta.json`).

## What this means for DamDays

- **The hard filter is a visible dam.** The farmers to approach early are those with at least one dam of 0.54 ha or more, and we already know where every such dam-sized waterbody is: 894 in NSW Central West and 789 in western Victoria / SE South Australia, mostly farm dams (not yet checked one by one against land use).
- **The default 3 km circle is bigger than most target farms** (2,827 ha, against a typical 300 to 1,500 ha). In set-up, the farmer should move the point and shrink the radius (the app allows both) so the text lists their own dams, not a neighbour's.
- **Say it plainly in the pitch** (suggested line): *DamDays is for family sheep and cattle farms, mostly 300 to 1,500 ha in south-eastern Australia's dam country, run by one or two people, whose stock drink from at least one dam big enough for the satellites to see.*

## Not verified, and open questions

- **Carting cost per kilolitre.** A water-tank seller's 2018 page quoted about $13.50 per kL in NSW, but the page could not be opened to check it, so it is not used here. We found no current government figure.
- **Mobile coverage at the house and in the ute,** which the weekly text needs: not researched.
- **Dams per farm.** There is no census of dams per property; the 8 to 17 per 1,000 ha are state averages over all land (derived).
- **How many target farms have a dam DamDays can see.** Our whole-box counts (about 1 in 11 points within 2 km) include non-farm land; the share for grazing farms is unknown.
- **Whether farms with a big dam are larger or richer than average.** Not measured; we have no property boundaries.
- **What farmers would pay.** Not researched (and the repository is public, so no pricing here).
- **ABARES's latest year (2024-25)** was added in June 2026 and may be revised.

- To recount the NSW Central West coverage from committed files (prints 7,897 and 15,461 of 90,000 points):

```
.venv/Scripts/python.exe -c "
import json, numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import cKDTree
d = json.load(open('app/data/real/forecasts.json'))['dams']
lat = np.array([x['lat'] for x in d]); lon = np.array([x['lon'] for x in d])
la0, la1, lo0, lo1 = -33.5, -30.5, 147.0, 150.0   # NSW Central West box (damdays/config.py)
k = 111.32 * np.cos(np.deg2rad((la0 + la1) / 2)); s = 0.01
g = np.array([(a, b) for a in np.arange(la0 + s/2, la1, s) for b in np.arange(lo0 + s/2, lo1, s)])
t = cKDTree(np.c_[lon * k, lat * 111.32])
for r in (2.0, 3.0):
    n = np.array([len(i) for i in t.query_ball_point(np.c_[g[:, 1] * k, g[:, 0] * 111.32], r)])
    print(r, 'km:', (n > 0).sum(), 'of', len(n), 'points')
"
```
