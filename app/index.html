About DamDays

How it works

  1. Satellites watch farm dams. Landsat has photographed Australia every couple of weeks since 1987. Geoscience Australia turns those photos into the wet area of each waterbody: 38 years of water history per dam.
  2. We learn how each dam behaves. How fast it drops in summer, how it fills after rain, and whether it runs drier than its neighbours.
  3. We forecast the chance of running low. For each dam: the chance it falls below a third of its usual full level in the next 90 days (written "3 in 10"), plus a whole six-month curve.
  4. The DamDays number: days of water left. The number of days the dam should stay above a third, 9 times in 10. If it says 60, then in 9 seasons out of 10 like this one the dam would last at least 60 days. It is the headline.
  5. One text a week. Farmers don't open apps or emails, so each farm gets one text message a week: the days of water left for the dams that matter most, how full they are, and which are already low. My farm shows the text for a homestead you pick on the map.
  6. The DamDays Rating. For lenders: before each season starts (1 July), a rating for every 2 km patch of farmland: the chance its dams all run dry between October and March.

A note on words. In DamDays "%" always means how full a dam is: its wet area against its usual full area. A chance is written "3 in 10". The test scores below are for judges and lenders, so they keep their usual units, each in plain words.

How we checked it

We wrote down the tests and the pass marks before writing any code (see PREREG.md in the repository). The model learned only from years before July 2016 and was then scored on July 2016 to June 2026, years it had never seen. A third region was locked away before the event and is opened only once, on camera, after the model is frozen. Proof shows the test years in pictures: what we said against what happened, year by year, and dam by dam.

Honest limits

  • Only dams larger than about 0.5 ha. A dam needs at least six 30 m satellite pixels. Bores, tanks and small dams are not visible.
  • Water area, not depth. Satellites see how much of the dam is wet, not how deep it is. "A third" means a third of the dam's usual full wet area.
  • Clouds and gaps. Some weeks have no clear look. Each forecast shows the date of the last clear look.
  • A chance is a chance. A chance of 3 in 10 means that about 3 of every 10 dams like this one fall below a third. Some will, most won't.
  • The days are cautious, not certain. "At least N days" held 9 times in 10 in the ten test years, so about 1 time in 10 a dam drops below a third sooner. Each forecast starts from the dam's last clear satellite look; the days since that look are taken off.
  • A farm is a circle. We have no property boundaries, so a farm's dams are the dams within the radius you choose. On a small block the circle can take in a neighbour's dams; on a big station it can miss some.
  • The rating ranks properties, not drought years. It is good at saying which farms are more exposed in a season. Forecasting whether a whole season will be dry is a different job, best left to climate outlooks.
  • Not advice. Use it alongside your own eyes on the dam and local knowledge.

Why it matters for COP31

  • Awareness track: helping farmers adapt. Farmers get a forecast for each dam the satellites can see, counted in days, to help them decide while there are still choices: sell or move stock, book water carting, or fix a leaking dam.
  • Global Goal on Adaptation. Its framework (the UAE Framework for Global Climate Resilience) includes targets on water scarcity and climate-resilient farming. DamDays gives a farm-by-farm measure of water security, updated every couple of weeks.
  • A number lenders could use for adaptation. Lenders and valuers could rate drought exposure property by property. Because the rating follows each dam's own record, a farm that improves its water supply can see that reflected over time, so adaptation can earn better finance.
